## Research Question

**Decision question.** Which public communities, platforms, fellowships, events and competitions give a GenAI job seeker the best chance of being noticed by people who hire, including people at frontier labs, and how should they take part? The answer informs D-005 and D-011.

**Reading chosen.** "Noticed" means that a person who hires can see something the job seeker made, or that a programme or community which hirers watch has taken them in. Where a documented route exists (a hire, an outcome figure, an employer statement), the report records it and grades it. Where none exists, the report says so and judges venues by access, cost and risk. Three readers are served: an engineer in India without US, UK or Canadian work authorisation (and without a brand-name employer), an engineer in the US or Europe, and a PhD researcher moving to industry.

**Sub-questions** (the brief's nine approach items, plus the eleven knowledge-base claims K1 to K11):

1. How AI researchers and engineers use X and its alternatives, and whether hires started there.
2. Which engagement tactics have evidence behind them.
3. The state of the communities directory, with dated activity and verdicts.
4. Fellowships and residencies: eligibility, geography, stipend, outcomes, status.
5. Events: hiring evidence, cost, visa considerations.
6. Competitions and leaderboards: named hires, costs.
7. India: venues and online communities.
8. Open source as a route: documented hires and contribution thresholds.
9. Paid groups, cohorts and scam reports.

**Scope as applied.** Temporal anchor 2026-11-11; evidence window 2024-01-01 to 2026-11-11 per the brief; pages were read through 2026-11-14. Three items older than 2026-01-01 are kept only as flagged stale: an OpenAI Residency article of 2021-11-30, a Meta AI Residency page that describes a 2023 cohort, and a Microsoft Research India page that describes a 2024 cycle. Covered: X, Bluesky and Threads (platform-level evidence only); fourteen community and platform entries; sixteen fellowship, residency and open-source mentorship programmes; four event entries; two competitions plus the GPU MODE kernel leaderboards and Anthropic's public take-home; and scam risk. Not reached, and listed under Absences: Kaggle, AIMO, NeurIPS competition tracks, Ray Summit, PyCon India, cities other than Bengaluru, and AI-specific Telegram or WhatsApp groups.

**Method.** I searched with WebSearch (standard mode), fetched the pages that searches surfaced with WebFetch (which returns a summarising extract and only accepts URLs that a search has returned), and read repository policy files from public git clones. 66 sources are in the ledger. Facts an organisation publishes about its own offering were taken from its own page. Outcome claims by programme owners and community owners were treated as selected success stories and marked COI.

## Recommendation

No source read measures a participation-to-hire rate (KF1), so these rules choose venues by access, cost, risk and whether a public artefact results. The grade on each rule describes the evidence behind the rule, not the chance of a job. Hours are judgement (D), because no source tested a weekly budget (K2). Each weekly routine below totals six hours.

### Rules for every reader

| # | Rule | Evidence | Grade | Failure mode and stop rule |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| R1 | Each week, make something a stranger can check: a merged pull request, a reproduction with code, a leaderboard entry or a short results write-up. Treat X, Bluesky and LinkedIn as distribution for it, not as the work. | Recruiting doors found are artefact-based: the take-home [S34] and vLLM's committer criteria [S28]; a named researcher advises public write-ups [S53]. | C | Failure: output nobody reads or checks. Stop rule: with no merged pull request, maintainer reply or leaderboard rank after 4 to 6 weeks, change project or venue (judgement, D). |
| R2 | Do not plan around a hidden-job-market figure. Keep applying publicly, and ask people who have worked with you for referrals. | Referrals are about 18% of hires but reach interview at 40% against 3% for inbound [S2, S16]; the 70 to 90% figure is untraceable [S1]. | B | Failure: no one is in a position to refer you. Stop rule: if no one can, rely on R1 and public applications. |
| R3 | Follow each project's rules: open an issue or RFC before a pull request, keep pull requests small, disclose AI help, and write your own descriptions and replies. | PyTorch, llama.cpp, Zed, Ghostty and vLLM policy files [S29, S30, S31, S32, S45]. | A | Failure: compliance does not secure a review. | R4 | Run coding tests from unknown recruiters in a sandbox, check the recruiter through the company's own channels, and never pay for a job or a "referral". Report fee demands to helpline 1930 or the National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal. | DC3 advisory of 2026-09-18 [S52]; CareerIndia of 2026-09-17 [S10]. | A | Failure: extra friction can slow a genuine process; accept that cost. |
| R5 | Pay for a community or mentor only when the product is the learning itself. Use free-to-start mentoring as feedback, not as a hiring door. | No independent job-outcome evidence for Maven, MentorCruise, Build Fast with AI or ADPList (see Absences) [S48, S49, S44, S54]. | C | Failure: free sessions can be generic. ### What would change these rules

- A community, fellowship or event that publishes hiring outcomes with denominators.
- First-person accounts from hiring managers who source from a venue.
- Evidence that a tactic on X or in a community leads to interviews.
- A change in programme status, such as Anthropic Fellows offering visa sponsorship or new MATS, ERA or SPAR windows.

### 7. Open source as a route

The brief asks for a refresh of the knowledge base's open-source thresholds, but it does not give those thresholds, so no comparison is made. The evidence found is as follows.

- **vLLM** describes committership as highly selective and merit-based. A committer typically meets at least two of four patterns: author of an accepted RFC that shaped direction; a measurable, widely adopted performance or reliability gain; long-term ownership of a subsystem; or significant ecosystem enablement work. No quantitative bar is set, but past committers submitted about 30+ substantial pull requests and reviewed about 10+ substantial external ones. Nominations go to a private committer list, with a two-week feedback period [S28].
- **llama.cpp** lists Collaborators with Triage rights, for people with significant contributions who review code they own [S30]. Its AI policy requires disclosure and bars AI-written communications.
- **PyTorch** requires an actionable issue for a pull request unless a maintainer pre-approves [S29].
- **Hires.** Two 2026 events are documented: the OpenClaw creator joining OpenAI [S6], and ggml.ai joining Hugging Face [S33]. Neither documents the pattern of contribution that precedes a hire, and neither is a base rate.

### 8. Paid groups, cohorts and scam risk

- **Paid cohort.** Maven's AI Evals cohort costs 4,500 USD for the live version, runs 33 live sessions over 11 lessons at 3 to 5 hours a week, includes a private Discord, mentions job leads and states no placement outcome [S48].
- **Mentoring.** MentorCruise lists 120 to 450 USD a month for plans [S49]. ADPList is free to start, and its mentor and member counts differ between its own pages [S50, S51].
- **Referral groups.** No AI-specific paid referral group was found. The scam explainer read lists unsolicited group additions, fee demands and changing payment IDs as signals, and names helpline 1930 and the National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal [S10].
- **Fake recruiters.** The DC3 advisory of 2026-09-18 describes a campaign that delivers malware through coding tests; it reports at least 30,000 devices compromised and about 10.71 million USD in crypto stolen from December 2025 to July 2026 [S52].
- **Regulation.** No Indian regulator action on placement claims by AI courses was found (see Absences).

## Discovered Concerns

1. **Fake-recruiter malware.** A US DC3 advisory of 2026-09-18 reports at least 30,000 devices compromised and about 10.71 million USD in crypto stolen from December 2025 to July 2026 through coding tests sent by fake recruiters [S52, S63]. This touches every reader who takes a take-home test and deserves a page of its own.
2. **Assessment integrity.** Anthropic's take-home was shortened from four hours to two after a model beat most humans, and first-day submissions below 1300 cycles were invalid because a model had edited the tests [S34]. Any employer that sets a take-home faces the same problem; candidates who alter tests lose the result.
3. **Agent-written pull requests.** Four repositories read on 2026-11-13 state rules against pull requests the author cannot explain or has not disclosed as AI-assisted, and one says undisclosed AI use may lead to a permanent ban [S30, S31, S32, S29]. Sending such pull requests to gain visibility can damage it instead.
4. **Country-tiered pay.** The same LFX Mentorship pays 1,300 USD in India and 6,000 USD in the US for programmes starting on or after 2026-07-01 [S22].
5. **Stale programme pages stay reachable.** OpenAI's residency article (2021-11-30), Meta's residency page (2023 cohort) and Microsoft Research India's page (2024 cycle) can be mistaken for open programmes [S25, S26, S27].
6. **Conflict of interest.** Anthropic, the developer of the model that wrote this report, hosts three programmes and the take-home that appear here. Their eligibility facts are primary (A) and their outcome claims are graded C.
7. **Pricing signal.** A single cohort fee of 4,500 USD for a six-week course [S48] is about 3.5 times the LFX stipend for India per programme (M: 4,500 divided by 1,300) [S22]; the two are unlike products, so the ratio is a scale comparison only.
